How to read TACoS without fooling yourself

TACoS, total advertising cost of sales, is ad spend divided by total sales, including the sales ads never touched. Where ACoS only asks what the ad spend bought, TACoS asks what advertising costs the whole account. It is the closest thing Amazon advertising has to an honesty metric, which is exactly why it deserves more care than it usually gets.

Read it carelessly and it will tell you three different lies, all of them plausible. We have watched each of them cost a founder real money.

What TACoS actually measures

Spend £2,000 on ads in a month where the account does £20,000 in total, and TACoS is 10%. ACoS, by contrast, divides the same £2,000 only by the £8,000 of sales that came from ads, and reports 25%. Same account, same spend, two different stories. ACoS measures efficiency inside the campaigns. TACoS measures advertising's weight on the whole business.

That second question is the one that matters when you are deciding how much to invest. Which is why a falling TACoS, held steady over months, is the single most reliable sign that a brand's flywheel is working: ads pull sales, sales lift rank, rank brings organic sales, and the ad load shrinks as a share of revenue.

The three traps

The scaling trap

A brand triples ad spend to grab share during a peak season. ACoS holds at 22%, so the account looks disciplined. TACoS rises from 9% to 14% because total sales did not triple along with the spend. Nothing broke, but the account is buying its growth, not compounding it, and someone should say so out loud.

The launch trap

A new ASIN launches with aggressive spend and thin total sales. TACoS hits 45% and panic sets in at the board meeting. But a launch is supposed to look like this: there is no organic base yet, so every sale is bought, and bought sales are the tuition for organic rank. Judge a launch by rank velocity and review rate, not by TACoS.

The harvest trap

The reverse error, and the more expensive one. A brand sees TACoS fall from 12% to 7% and declares victory, so it cuts spend. Two quarters later organic rank has slipped, sessions have fallen with it, and total sales are down 18%. The falling TACoS was real, but it was a warning about under-investment, not a trophy.

A metric is doing its job when it can disappoint you. If TACoS can only ever go down, it is decoration, not measurement.

How we read it

We run every account against a simple grid. TACoS never appears in it alone; it always appears next to the thing that gives it meaning.

Reading TACoS against its context
PatternTACoS alone saysWith context, it usually means
Rising, sales flatAd problemStructure has decayed; restructure before raising budgets
Rising, sales rising fasterScalingDeliberate share-buying; set an exit date and an exit number
Falling, rank steadyFlywheel workingHold spend, defend rank, bank the margin
Falling, rank slippingEfficiencyOver-harvesting; reinvest before the organic base erodes

The 30-day cadence we run

Every retained account gets the same monthly rhythm, and TACoS sits inside it rather than on top of it:

  • Week 1: read TACoS against rank and sessions, not against last month's spend.
  • Week 2: harvest, moving proven search terms to exact match and starving the terms that never convert.
  • Week 3: test, one structural change per campaign family, so cause and effect stay legible.
  • Week 4: rebalance, shifting budget toward the ASINs whose organic rank the ads are actually building.

Worth writing down

TACoS is a direction, not a destination. Set it a target range with your accountant, from contribution margin. Ours usually land between 6% and 12%. Judge it quarterly, with rank and sessions beside it.

If your account's TACoS has been drifting and nobody can say why, that question is answerable. It is usually the first thing our audit answers, in the first week.

Key takeaways

  • TACoS weighs advertising on the whole account; ACoS only scores the campaigns.
  • Rising TACoS with flat sales means structure, not budget.
  • Launches are allowed a high TACoS. Judge them on rank velocity instead.
  • Falling TACoS with slipping rank is a warning to reinvest, not a win.
  • Read it monthly, judge it quarterly, and never read it alone.
